Slide 1 — Title  (15 sec)
                                                                                                                          "This is the Kickstarter Pre-Launch Recommender. It predicts whether a campaign will succeed before it launches, and
  turns that prediction into specific advice the creator can act on."

  [click]

  ---
  Slide 2 — Problem  (40 sec)

  "About 40% of Kickstarter campaigns fail. Existing tools that try to predict success give you a single number — 'you
  have a 32% chance' — which doesn't tell you what to fix.

  The goal here is two things: estimate success probability, and give creator-level, feature-level advice on what to
  change. The hard constraint: predictions only use what a creator controls at launch. No post-outcome features, no
  curatorial signals like staff picks."

  [click]

  ---
  Slide 3 — Data  (50 sec)

  "Data comes from WebRobots — they've been scraping Kickstarter monthly since 2009. After dedup and keeping only
  resolved campaigns, I have 334,080 rows.

  Critical detail: the split is time-based, not random. Train on everything before 2022, test on 2022 onward. That's
  236k train, 97k test. A random split would leak future information into training and inflate metrics — you'd be using
  post-2022 data to predict pre-2022 outcomes, which doesn't match how anyone would use this in production.

  Feature space: 52 tabular dimensions plus 596,000 TF-IDF features from the blurb."

  [click]

  ---
  Slide 4 — Leakage check  (45 sec)

  "First model I trained scored AUROC 1.0. That's not a win, that's a bug.

  The flag is_spotlight had correlation 1.0 with the label — turns out Kickstarter only sets that flag after a campaign
  succeeds. Strictly post-outcome.

  I also dropped is_staff_pick — not strictly post-outcome, but curatorial. Not something a creator controls, so it
  doesn't belong in a pre-launch tool.

  Final feature set: 52 tabular plus 596k TF-IDF, all controllable at launch. Worth showing this slide because catching
  the leak is the methodology — anyone who reports an AUROC over 0.95 on this dataset hasn't done the leakage check."

  [click]

  ---
  Slide 5 — Models  (60 sec)

  "Three models. Logistic regression on the 52 tabular features as the floor — AUROC 0.773.

  XGBoost on the same 52 tabular features — 0.826. That's the deployed model, the one in the demo.

  XGBoost with all 596k features — 0.868. Headline number, but too large to deploy on a free tier.

  The full model used QuantileDMatrix to fit the sparse text features into 12 GB of GPU memory. Standard DMatrix tried
  to allocate 26 GB and OOM'd.

  Take-away: text adds about 4 AUROC points over tabular alone. It helps, but the bulk of the signal is in goal,
  duration, category, country, and timing."

  [click]

  ---
  Slide 6 — SHAP recommendations  (55 sec)

  "This is the contribution that distinguishes this tool from existing predictors.

  I use XGBoost's built-in tree SHAP to get per-feature attributions for each prediction. Then I translate those
  attributions into plain-language advice. Two guardrails: a recommendation only fires when SHAP for that feature is
  below -0.05 — meaningfully hurting the prediction — and when the suggestion makes sense for the current value.

  The second guardrail matters. Without it, the model would say 'add a video' to a campaign that already has one. The
  advice has to be valid given the current state of the campaign.

  Sample output is on the slide — 'your goal of $250,000 may be too high', 'a 60-day campaign hurts your odds, most run
  21 to 35 days'."

  [click]

  ---
  Slide 7 — Temporal drift  (45 sec)

  "I trained the same model on four sliding time windows and tested each one on post-2022 data.

  The 2010–2014 model hits 0.80 AUROC on its own holdout but drops to 0.64 on 2022 campaigns. That's a 16-point gap. The
   2021+ model actually does better on 2022+ than on its own holdout — distribution is closest.

  Take-away: a model trained on a decade-old data is unsafe on today's campaigns. Kickstarter's distribution has
  shifted. Anyone deploying this in production needs a retraining cadence — at least every 12 months."

  [click]

  ---
  Slide 8 — Trends ablation  (45 sec)

  "This slide is here on purpose. I added a Google Trends momentum signal as a feature, hypothesizing that campaigns
  launching during topic spikes would do better.

  Ablation: full model with Trends, 0.868. Full model without Trends, 0.869. The signal contributes essentially nothing
  on test data.

  I'm reporting it because it didn't work. Plausible hypotheses sometimes don't pan out, and burying that would be
  misleading. The right next step would be a query-specific Trends lookup rather than the generic one I used — there's
  still a plausible version of this signal that would help."

  [click]

  Slide 10 — Lebanon case study  (60 sec)

  "The proposal called for a Lebanon angle — Lebanese-diaspora creators using crowdfunding for relief, cultural, or
  reconstruction projects.

  The keyword filter caught 49 campaigns. That's too small for reliable subgroup metrics — the 95% confidence interval
  on AUROC at N=49 spans 0.66 to 0.93, which is uninformative. So I treated it qualitatively.

  Three case types on the slide. The correct risk flag — a Lebanese cookbook with $50k goal, no video, 7-word blurb.
  Model gave it 2% probability and flagged all three issues. Actually failed.

  The high-confidence win — Automne au Levant, a comic from France. Goal $3k, short campaign, video. Model: 96%.
  Succeeded.

  And a humility check — some campaigns succeed despite low scores because community trust and pre-launch outreach live
  outside the feature set. A low score is a signal to investigate, not a verdict.

  Full appendix has 5 real walk-throughs and 3 hypothetical personas."

  [click]

  ---
  Slide 11 — Limitations  (50 sec)

  "Five things this tool is not.

  One — correlational, not causal. SHAP tells me which features the model used; it doesn't tell me changing them would
  change the outcome.

  Two — per-group AUROC ranges 0.69 to 0.90 across categories and countries. Not equally calibrated everywhere.

  Three — base-rate shift. Post-2022 campaigns succeed 72% in this scrape vs 61% pre-2022. Could be platform changes,
  could be scraping selection bias. I don't claim to know.

  Four — the deployed model is 0.826, not the headline 0.868 number. Different models for different constraints.

  Five — threshold. I tuned to a balanced operating point at p = 0.64. A different threshold is appropriate if
  false-positive cost differs from false-negative cost."

  [click]

  ---
  Slide 12 — Q&A / next steps  (40 sec)

  "What I'd do next:

  Calibrate per-category — the 0.69 to 0.90 spread means a single global threshold is the wrong call for some segments.

  Re-test the Trends signal with a query-specific lookup. The current ablation may have under-powered it.

  Causal layer — replace SHAP-based recommendations with an uplift model trained on near-duplicate campaigns that differ
   only in one feature. That would let me make causal claims rather than correlational ones.

  Productionize the retraining cadence at 12 months minimum.

  Questions?"

  ---
  Anticipated Q&A — quick answers

  - "How do you know the recommendations actually help?" → I don't, and the limitations slide is explicit about that.
  The recommendations are derived from patterns in successful campaigns. To know if changing a feature changes the
  outcome, you'd need an experiment or an uplift model on near-duplicate pairs. That's the causal layer in next steps.
  - "Why XGBoost over a neural net?" → On 596k sparse features and 236k rows, gradient-boosted trees outperform neural
  nets in most published benchmarks. XGBoost also gives me free SHAP via tree attribution, which is the foundation of
  the recommendations. A neural net would need a separate SHAP/LIME pass.
  - "Why is base rate higher post-2022?" → Two competing explanations: platform-level changes, or scraping bias toward
  already-resolved newer campaigns. I genuinely don't know which. Reporting it as an open question.
  - "Is the Lebanon analysis statistically meaningful?" → No, and that's stated in the limitations. N=49 makes any
  subgroup metric noisy. The case study is qualitative — the value is showing the recommender produces sensible advice
  for under-represented profiles, not proving subgroup accuracy.
  - "Why does temporal drift cut so hard for the 2010 model?" → Kickstarter changed. Categories were added, the audience
   shifted from tech-bro early adopters to general consumers, base rates moved. A feature like 'category=Technology'
  meant something different in 2012 than in 2024.
